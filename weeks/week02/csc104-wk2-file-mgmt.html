Why bother with file management?

Every file you create or download lands somewhere. Without a system, your computer turns into a digital junk drawer — and the problem grows quietly until the day you need something and cannot find it. In the video I look at my own drive with a program called WinDirStat: 1.6 million files, about a million of them inside a single user folder, spread across six drives. Search alone does not rescue you at that scale.

Saves time

You stop hunting for the one file you know you saved somewhere. Structure lets you narrow a search to one folder instead of scanning every drive.

Gives you confidence

When a search returns fifty results, you still do not know if the right one is in there. When you know where a file lives, you know you found it.

Builds a habit that scales

You may only have a couple hundred files today. That changes. Good habits now cost nothing; retrofitting them onto a million files costs a weekend.

Archive, don't hoard

Keeping old work is fine — keeping it in the middle of your active folders is not. Move finished projects into an Archive folder or onto an external drive so your working folders stay short enough to read at a glance.

Four habits that do most of the work

None of these are graded. They are the whole payoff of this week, and they take about five minutes to adopt.

1. Use a clear folder structure

Broad categories first — School, Work, Photos — then specific subfolders inside them. Think of a filing cabinet: drawers, then folders, then papers.

2. Name files consistently

Pick a pattern and stick to it. YYYY-MM-DD_Project-Name.docx works well, because dates written that way sort in date order. Always use two digits: 2026-09-13, not 2026-9-13.

3. Keep the desktop clear

The desktop is for what you are working on right now, not for storage. Once about twenty things pile up there, you cannot find any of them.

4. Put things away when you are done

The Downloads folder is where files land, not where they live. Move a file into its real folder as soon as you know you are keeping it.

Step 2 — Take a screenshot that shows the folders

Windows: the Snipping Tool

Search the Start menu for Snipping Tool and open it. Click New. The screen grays out. Click and drag a box around the part of File Explorer you want, then save the image.

Mac: Shift-Command-4

Hold Shift and Command, then press 4. Click one corner of the area you want and drag to the opposite corner. The image saves to your desktop.

Why the partial screenshot? On a Mac, Shift-Command-3 captures the entire screen, so your folders end up tiny and hard to read once I open the file. Cropping to just the Finder or File Explorer window means I can actually see what you built.

Step 3 — Upload it to Canvas

Submit the image file to the File Management Assignment in the Week 2 module. That is the whole submission — no document, no write-up.

Doing it on your platform

The textbook is written for Windows. The Mac has the same capabilities in Finder — the controls just sit in different places. Pick your platform below.

Getting around File Explorer

  • Making a folder: open the folder you want it inside, then New → Folder on the toolbar, or right-click empty space → New → Folder. Type the name and press Enter.
  • Renaming: select the folder and press F2, or right-click → Rename.
  • Moving and copying: drag to move within the same drive; hold Ctrl while dragging to copy. Cut, copy and paste work on files and folders exactly as they do on text.
  • Quick Access: pin the folders you use every day to the top of the sidebar. Right-click a folder → Pin to Quick access. Your CSC 104 folder is a good candidate.
  • Details and preview panes: the View menu turns on a pane that shows size and dates, or previews a file's contents without opening it.
  • Saving to the cloud: OneDrive appears in the sidebar like any other folder. Saving into it means the file is on the drive and backed up.

Getting around Finder

  • Making a folder: open the folder you want it inside, then File → New Folder, or press Shift-Command-N. Type the name and press Return.
  • Renaming: select the folder and press Return, then type the new name.
  • Moving and copying: drag to move within the same drive; hold Option while dragging to copy. Command-C then Option-Command-V moves a file rather than copying it.
  • Tags: color-coded labels you can put on any file regardless of where it lives, then search for later.
  • Quick Look: select a file and press the spacebar for a full-size preview without opening the app.
  • Sidebar favorites: drag a folder into the Finder sidebar to keep it one click away.
  • Saving to the cloud: iCloud Drive appears in the sidebar; OneDrive and Google Drive do too once installed.
